# Low-resolution plate benchmark

Generated by `scripts/lr_benchmark.py`. Regenerate, do not hand-edit:

```
python scripts/lr_benchmark.py --heights 16,20,24 --frames 8 --tracks 20 --severity night
```

## Why this file exists

The wide-area cameras on the Sentinel grid put a number plate into 20-30 px of frame. That is
not a corner case, it is the operating point the evaluation described, so it needs a number
rather than an opinion.

The published benchmark for exactly this regime is **UFPR-SR-Plates** (Nascimento, Lima,
Ribeiro, Schwartz, Laroca and Menotti, *Toward Advancing License Plate Super-Resolution in
Real-World Scenarios: A Dataset and Benchmark*, Journal of the Brazilian Computer Society
31:1, 2025). Their low-resolution plates have a **median height of 18-21 px**, and they report:

| condition | recognition |
|---|---|
| LR crop straight to OCR | 1.7 - 2.2 % |
| + best single-image super-resolution (LCDNet) | 29.9 - 31.1 % |
| + majority vote by character position over 5 super-resolved images | **42.3 - 44.7 %** |

Two things in that paper shape our design. First, the jump from 31 % to 45 % comes from
combining several reconstructions rather than trusting one - which is what `mvcp.py`
implements. Second, **they excluded night footage from the dataset entirely**, because
infrared interference made plates hard to read "even in HR images". Our grid is night footage.

## What is measured

Three conditions on the same degraded frames:

- **LR -> OCR** - the sharpest low-res crop, upscaled only enough for the reader to accept it.
  This is what a naive pipeline does.
- **single-image SR** - learned super-resolution on the sharpest frame, then the full
  enhancement chain. This is what most published pipelines do.
- **ours** - `mfsr.super_resolve` multi-frame reconstruction at three scales, plus fusion,
  learned SR, plain Lanczos and the raw enhanced crop, all read by all four OCR engines, and
  decided by grammar-aware majority vote per character position (`mvcp.decode_track`).

Scoring is exact full-plate match. A plate with one wrong character counts as wrong.

Degradation is applied in the order a camera applies it - fixed viewing angle, contrast
collapse toward the local mean, linear motion blur, optical downscale, Poisson-Gaussian sensor
noise, JPEG. A plain downscale is not used, because that is the specific criticism the paper
levels at earlier work: rendered glyphs at full contrast survive a downscale, and a benchmark
built that way reports accuracy the field does not have.

## Results

### night severity — the honest comparison

Contrast 0.30, heavy motion blur, sensor noise sigma 14, JPEG q35. Calibrated so the LR
baseline lands at the paper's 1.7-2.2 %.

| plate height | LR -> OCR | single-image SR | **ours: MFSR ensemble + MVCP** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16 px | 0.0 % | 0.0 % | **5.0 %** |
| 20 px | 0.0 % | 0.0 % | **65.0 %** |
| 24 px | 0.0 % | 15.0 % | **95.0 %** |

### dusk severity

Contrast 0.55, moderate blur, sigma 9, JPEG q45.

| plate height | LR -> OCR | single-image SR | **ours: MFSR ensemble + MVCP** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16 px | 20.0 % | 10.0 % | **55.0 %** |
| 20 px | 55.0 % | 50.0 % | **85.0 %** |
| 24 px | 65.0 % | 70.0 % | **95.0 %** |

20 distinct plates x 8 degraded frames per track, seed 7.

## What the numbers say

**The ensemble vote is the whole difference.** At 20 px night, one reconstruction reads
nothing and several reconstructions read 65 %. That is the same effect the paper measures
(31 % -> 45 %) and it is why the ensemble deliberately contains a plain Lanczos control
alongside the learned paths: a character that only the learned path produces is an artefact,
a character all of them produce was photographed.

**Single-image SR is not reliably better than doing nothing.** At dusk it scores *below* the
LR baseline at 16 px (10 % vs 20 %) and 20 px (50 % vs 55 %). It hallucinates about as often as
it helps, which is precisely why a pipeline that super-resolves once and reads once should not
be trusted with a plate an officer will act on.

**There is a cliff just under 20 px.** 65 % at 20 px, 5 % at 16 px. The plate face is about
65 % glyph, so that is roughly 13 px of glyph working and 10 px failing - consistent with the
CRNN/SVTR recognisers being trained on 32-48 px text lines. `preprocess.GLYPH_MARGINAL_PX` is
set from this measurement: below ~10 px of glyph the pipeline refuses and reports "plate not
resolvable at this camera" rather than guessing.

**The absolute dusk numbers are optimistic.** Our source plates are rendered glyphs at clean
contrast, and rendered glyphs survive downscaling better than photographed ones - the LR
baseline reading 55 % at 20 px where the paper's real-world LR reads 1.7 % is the tell. The
night rows are calibrated against that baseline and are the ones to quote; the dusk rows show
the shape of the curve. The finding that does not depend on the calibration is the *relative*
lift, which is 2-3x over both baselines at every size and severity tested.
